Two things happened in AI between September 14 and September 20. Each one is worth five minutes if you run a business.

One is about who gets to set the pace of AI development. The other is about what AI can actually do once a customer starts talking.

## Washington pushed back on slowing AI development

The debate started before the week did. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei suggested slowing progress on the most powerful AI systems so safety work could catch up. His proposal included independent evaluators inside AI companies, shared standards, and international cooperation. He drew a line between that and stopping technical progress.

On Friday, four paying subscribers sued Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceXAI. They argued that planning a slowdown broke antitrust law and lowered the value of their subscriptions. The complaint is about competitors coordinating, not about one company being careful on its own. These are allegations, not a ruling. The Associated Press reported the suit.

On Saturday, President Trump opposed a slowdown and said he planned an "AI Force" and an AI czar. CBS News reported the remarks.

## What this means for your business

This week did not settle how fast AI will develop, or how it will be regulated.

That distinction matters. Arguing about the next generation of AI is not the same decision as whether today's tools can help your business.

You can already check whether AI saves your office time — preparing quotes, organizing customer information, drafting follow-ups. You do not need that debate to finish first. The same is true of [the camera button most people never press](/resources/ai-camera-button-photo-to-text-for-contractors): it works on the phone you already have.

Pick one recurring task. Measure how long it takes now. Try the tool, review the result, and count the time spent fixing mistakes.

That gives you evidence about your team, not a prediction about the industry. If you want a way to choose which task to start with, [the first workflow worth automating](/resources/first-workflow-to-automate-for-contractors) is the filter we use.

## Google introduced voice AI that can switch languages mid-conversation

On Tuesday, Google introduced Gemini 3.8 Live and Gemini 3.8 Live Extended Thinking.

Google says the models support 97 languages, can switch languages inside one conversation, can use live visual input, and can keep a conversation going while they work on something else in the background. Rollouts cover developer tools and consumer products, and availability differs across them.

Google also highlights accuracy on combinations of letters and numbers — confirmation codes, claim numbers. That is a stated capability. It is not a guarantee that every detail lands correctly on a real customer call.

## What this means if you take calls in more than one language

If customers start in English and switch to Spanish halfway through, this is the shape of product worth testing — an AI receptionist included.

The model is only one piece of the system you are buying.

A call has to go right from start to finish: understand the request, record the right information, check availability, and hand the call to a person when it should.

Before you buy, ask the vendor for a recording of a call in which the customer:

- Switches languages halfway through
- Corrects their address or phone number
- Asks for an appointment that is not available
- Asks to speak with a person

Then look at what landed in your calendar or customer record. A natural-sounding conversation only helps if the work it produces is accurate.

## What to do with this week's news

The policy debate is still open. Google's release is another set of capabilities you can test, not a reason to rebuild the office around an announcement.

You do not need to follow every one. You do need a way to decide which ones matter.

Start with a problem your team already has. Test whether a tool helps. Expand when the result justifies it.

> If someone offers an AI receptionist, ask whether you can try it on a few of the hard calls your team already gets — and then check what it writes down. That tells you more than a polished demo.
