Three things happened in AI between October 12th and the 18th that are worth five minutes of your time if you run a trade or home service business.

None of them are product launches. All three are about what happens after the launch: who answers for AI, what it costs, and what it can break.

## Wednesday: Eight states sued OpenAI, and the FTC asked for its records

On Wednesday, eight states filed separate lawsuits against OpenAI in their own courts: Arizona, Arkansas, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska, and West Virginia. They allege that OpenAI misled people about how safe ChatGPT is, including for teenagers, and that the chatbot is built to be agreeable so users will trust it. The states want changes to how ChatGPT works, plus penalties and damages. OpenAI said it works with outside experts who review ChatGPT's behavior.

The same day, the Federal Trade Commission sent OpenAI and Anthropic formal demands for documents, more than 50 pages each, Axios reported. The FTC wants records on safety incidents, safeguards, marketing materials, and privacy policies. A demand like this is evidence gathering, not a finding that anyone broke the law. OpenAI said it would work constructively with the FTC.

**What that tells you:** the pressure on AI companies is coming through ordinary consumer protection law, not new AI rules. Nebraska's attorney general put it plainly: the lawsuit "simply holds OpenAI to the same standards" as any other company.

Notice that the FTC asked for marketing materials. The question is whether what a company says matches what its product does. You already live by that rule. "Licensed and insured" and "same day service" have to be true. The same goes for AI. If your website says an AI assistant answers every call or quotes jobs accurately, make sure it actually does.

## Wednesday: A TV show lost a finished season, and the backup didn't work

On Wednesday, the Swedish newspaper Aftonbladet reported that the fully edited new season of The Challenge Sverige, a reality show due to air November 22, had been wiped from the production company's server. Its sources said the deletion happened after the production company, Mastiff, gave an AI agent access to the server.

The broadcaster, TV4, disputes that. It says its own investigation points to human error at Mastiff, not AI. Both sides agree on the rest: the edited season is gone, the backup did not work, and the raw footage survived. The premiere has moved to 2027 while the season is recut from scratch.

**What that tells you:** whether software or a person deleted the files, the real failure is the same: a backup nobody had tested. Think about what you'd lose. Job photos, signed estimates, permits, warranty records, years of customer history. AI tools can now delete things as easily as a person can. Before you give one access to your job management software or shared drive, make sure you could get everything back.

## Thursday: Accenture capped its staff at $29 a month of AI

On Thursday, 404 Media reported that Accenture, one of the world's biggest consulting firms, now pays for only $29 a month of Claude use per employee. Some staff had been running up $500 to $1,000 a month. Anything over $29 has to be charged to a specific project's budget, and at least one employee lost access entirely. The internal memo told staff that "all costs are consumption-based."

An Accenture spokesperson said the firm's approach is to provide "the right tool for the right work" and that it keeps investing in AI. Accenture also tells its clients to invest heavily in AI.

**What that tells you:** the $20 monthly plan is not the whole story of what AI can cost. Flat monthly plans are predictable. Plans billed by how much you use them can climb fast. Some AI phone answering services and some office tools bill this way, so a busy storm season or a big week of estimates can mean a bigger bill. Before you sign up, ask one question: is this a flat price, or does it go up with use?

## Putting it together

Regulators started asking AI companies to back up their safety claims. A production company found out its backup was a guess. And one of the biggest consulting firms in the world put its own staff on an AI budget.

> The question stopped being whether AI can do the work. It became who answers for it, what it costs, and whether you can undo it.

None of this is a reason to stay away. It is a reason to know three things about any AI tool you use: what you're telling customers it does, what it's allowed to touch, and how you're paying for it.

P.S. Pick one job folder this week and try to restore it from your backup. If you can't, you found out on a quiet day instead of the day it matters.
