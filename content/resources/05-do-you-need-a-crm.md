## The graveyard problem

Most contracting and trade businesses have already bought a CRM once. It lasted a quarter. Now it's a $79-a-month contact list with jobs from two bid seasons ago still marked "pending."

The failure is almost never the software. It's that the CRM depended on someone typing things into it by hand, and nobody on a crew, in a truck, or running estimates all day actually does that consistently. Any system that requires discipline to stay accurate loses to the phone, the job site, and the day.

So the real question isn't "which CRM?" It's "what would keep a job pipeline accurate without anyone maintaining it?"

## Three signs you actually need one

**1. Leads slip.** An inquiry comes in while you're on a roof, mid-install, or between job sites, and by the time you call back they've hired someone else. If you can name a job you lost to slow follow-up in the last month, that's the sign.

**2. Follow-up lives in someone's head.** Quotes with no answer, jobs due for a seasonal check-in, past customers worth a call for maintenance — if chasing these depends on someone remembering, revenue is leaking on a schedule.

**3. Nobody can see the pipeline.** If "what's on the schedule next month?" requires calling three people or checking three phones, you don't have a pipeline — you have three private ones.

Zero of three? You don't need a CRM yet. Save the money.

## What actually makes a CRM work

The CRMs that survive on a job site have one thing in common: **the data enters and updates itself.**

| Manual CRM (dies) | Connected job system (lives) |
|---|---|
| Someone types in each new lead | Web forms, calls, and texts create the job record automatically |
| Someone remembers to follow up | Follow-up sequences fire on their own |
| Someone updates the job stage | Stage moves when the quote sends or the invoice pays |
| Reports built by hand, monthly | Job pipeline is live, always |

This is exactly the pattern behind [South Central Investment Group](/resources/south-central-investment-group-general-contractor-case-study), a general contracting operation that replaced calls, texts, and spreadsheets with one centralized system and cut daily admin from 3–4 hours to under 1. The CRM wasn't the value — the automated intake and follow-up wired into it was.

## Off-the-shelf or custom?

Honest rule of thumb:

- **Off-the-shelf** field-service platforms fit when your job flow looks like most other contractors in your trade: lead → estimate → schedule → invoice. Buy it, wire it to your intake, done.
- **Custom** fits when the workflow *is* the business — multi-phase GC jobs with subcontractor coordination, HVAC quoting with manufacturer-specific warranty logic, or a maintenance-plan business with recurring seasonal touchpoints. Forcing those into a generic pipeline is how CRMs end up abandoned by month three.

Either way, the wiring matters more than the brand. An off-the-shelf CRM connected to your intake and follow-up beats a "better" CRM standing alone, every time.
